## 5. Research

### 5.1 The fields, in one line each
- **Behavioral economics:** economics that accounts for how people really decide,
  with limited willpower, biased beliefs and habits, instead of perfectly rational choices.
- **Health economics:** how people, firms and governments produce, pay for and
  invest in health.
- **Where they meet (the focus):** why people under-invest in their own health, and
  which incentives, defaults, environments, technologies and policies change that.

### 5.2 Core ideas, in plain words

| Term | Plain version |
|---|---|
| Present bias | Today-you wins every argument with future-you. |
| Overconfidence / naiveté | We think future-us will be more disciplined than we are. |
| Commitment device | Locking in a decision before your willpower gets a vote. |
| Loss aversion | Losing $10 hurts more than winning $10 feels good. |
| Habit formation | Doing it enough times that it stops needing a decision. |
| Fresh start effect | Why Monday, the 1st and birthdays feel like a reset. |
| Temptation bundling | Only letting yourself have the fun thing while doing the hard thing. |
| Peer effects | You copy the people around you more than you think. |
| Health capital | Your health is an asset. It wears down, and you can invest in it. |
| Substitutes vs. complements | Does one health investment (a drug) replace another (exercise), or make it easier? |
| Selection bias | The people who signed up were already different. |
| Correlation vs. causation | "Linked to" isn't the same as "caused by." |
| Natural experiment | Something random did the experiment for us. |
| Randomized trial | Flip a coin for who gets it, then compare. |
| Opportunity cost | What you gave up to do it. |

### 5.3 Foundational literature (checked)

| Study | What it found |
|---|---|
| Grossman, *Journal of Political Economy* (1972) | Health as a capital stock: you're born with some, it depreciates with age, and you invest in it with time and money. The founding model of health economics. |
| DellaVigna & Malmendier, "Paying Not to Go to the Gym," *AER* (2006) | 7,752 members of three US gyms. People on $70+/month plans went 4.3 times a month, paying over $17 a visit when a 10-visit pass cost $10. They gave up about $600 on average. |
| Charness & Gneezy, "Incentives to Exercise," *Econometrica* (2009) | Students paid $100 to go 8 times in a month went more than twice as often in the 7 weeks after payments stopped. The effect came entirely from people who weren't regular gym-goers before. |
| Royer, Stehr & Sydnor, *AEJ: Applied* (2015) | Paying employees per gym visit left only small lasting effects. The ~12% who took a commitment contract kept going long after. |
| Acland & Levy, *Management Science* (2015) | People greatly overpredicted their future gym attendance. (Its habit result was disputed in a 2019 comment. Cite only the overprediction finding.) |
| Jones, Molitor & Reif, Illinois Workplace Wellness Study, *QJE* (2019) | Randomized trial, 4,834 employees: no significant effect on medical spending, health behaviors or productivity after more than two years. Participants were already healthier (selection). |
| Song & Baicker, *JAMA* (2019) | BJ's Wholesale (20 clubs vs. 140, about 33,000 workers): better self-reported habits after 18 months, no difference in clinical measures, spending or missed work. |
| Milkman et al., "Megastudies," *Nature* (2021) | 61,293 gym members, 54 four-week programs. 45% raised visits by 9–27%. Best: small rewards for coming back after a missed workout (+27%). Only 8% had effects that lasted after the program ended. |
| Milkman, Minson & Volpp, *Management Science* (2014) | Gym-only access to page-turner audiobooks raised visits 51% *at first*. The effect faded. |
| Dai, Milkman & Riis, *Management Science* (2014) | Gym visits rise at the start of a week, a semester and after a birthday (the fresh start effect). |
| Patel et al., *Annals of Internal Medicine* (2016) | 7,000-step goal: people who could *lose* money hit it on 45% of days, vs. 35% for those who earned money and 30% with no incentive. Effects faded after payments stopped. |
| Volpp et al., *JAMA* (2008) | Weight-loss incentives: about 13–14 lb lost in 16 weeks vs. about 4 lb for controls. Not sustained at 7 months. |
| Aral & Nicolaides, *Nature Communications* (2017) | About 1.1 million runners. Using weather as a natural experiment: when friends ran 1 km more, a person ran about 0.3 km more. |
| Lally et al., *European Journal of Social Psychology* (2010) | Habit automaticity took a median of 66 days (range 18–254; the longest are model estimates from an 84-day study). |
| Gibson & Shrader, *Review of Economics and Statistics* (2018) | Using sunset times: one more hour of average **weekly** sleep raised earnings about 1.1% short run and 5% long run. |
| Kosteas, *Journal of Labor Research* (2012) | Regular exercise (3+ times a week) linked to 6–10% higher wages. A correlation, not proven cause. |
| Ding et al., *The Lancet* (2016) | Physical inactivity cost about INT$67.5 billion worldwide in 2013. |
| Kaestner, Darden & Lakdawalla, *Journal of Health Economics* (2014) | Statin use linked to higher BMI and 20–33% higher odds of obesity. Evidence that the drug substitutes for healthy behavior. |
| Sugiyama et al., *JAMA Internal Medicine* (2014) | Statin users' calorie intake rose 9.6% over a decade; non-users' didn't. |

### 5.4 What's crowded (avoid as a main question)
- Paying people to exercise.
- Reminders and nudges (mostly small, fading effects; a 2026 *Economica* paper on
  20,187 users of the walking app WeWard found reminders raised app opens but not steps).
- Workplace wellness program trials.
- How long habits take to form.
- Sugar taxes and food deserts.

### 5.5 White space (open directions, not a plan)
Each needs a proper literature check (Google Scholar "Cited by," and a professor)
before committing. These are starting points.

| Direction | The puzzle | Free or accessible data | Difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|
| **GLP-1 drugs and exercise** | A 2026 study of 1,950 adults with fitness-tracker data (presented at ENDO 2026) found steps fell from 5,047 to 4,487 a day after starting a GLP-1. Industry surveys say users spend *more* on fitness. Are the drugs substitutes for exercise or complements? Economists have studied GLP-1s and jobs, grocery spending and mental health, but apparently not exercise. | Understanding America Study (USC survey with GLP-1 questions and fitness-tracker data; check access); NIH All of Us (needs the school to be registered) | Medium–hard |
| **Remote work and exercise** | Working from home saves commute time, yet a 2026 Finnish study of 99 hybrid workers found 45 more minutes of sitting on remote days. Does remote work raise or lower exercise, and for whom? | American Time Use Survey (free via IPUMS) | Low–medium |
| **Gym supply and activity** | Do new commercial gyms, especially low-cost chains, cause people nearby to exercise more, or just move existing gym-goers around? | Census County Business Patterns (fitness centers by county) + CDC PLACES (county activity) | Medium–hard |
| **Schedule shocks and habits** | Exercise drops during exams. Does it bounce back, or does the habit break for good? | Requires collecting data (survey or wearables) and ethics approval | Medium |

Other areas worth watching: health technology and wearables, sleep and productivity,
heat and physical activity, the economics of fitness as a status good, and
insurance and employer incentives.

### 5.6 Useful free datasets
- **American Time Use Survey (ATUS)** via IPUMS: minutes per activity on one day, including exercise and where people worked.
- **BRFSS** (CDC): yearly health survey, including physical activity.
- **NHANES** (CDC): health exams, diet, accelerometer data in some years.
- **NHIS** (CDC): national health interview survey.
- **CDC PLACES:** county and city health estimates.
- **County Business Patterns** (Census): businesses by type and county.
- **Understanding America Study** (USC): panel survey, with GLP-1 and fitness-tracker data (access terms vary).
- **Google Trends:** search interest over time and place.

### 5.7 Ways to do research (definitions)

| Route | What it is | Credit? |
|---|---|---|
| **Independent working paper** | A full paper written on your own and posted publicly (SSRN, personal site, LinkedIn). No permission needed to start. | No |
| **Honors thesis** | A long paper (often 30–60 pages) over one or two semesters with a faculty advisor. Can lead to "graduated with honors." Often has GPA or program requirements. | Yes |
| **Independent study** | A one-semester course you design with a professor; graded on a project. Needs a supervisor and a form before registration closes. | Yes |
| **Research assistant (RA)** | Helping a professor with their research, sometimes paid. Strongest route to a recommendation letter. | Sometimes |
| **Class paper** | Using a question for a final project in a class you're already taking. | Yes |
| **Undergraduate research grant / symposium** | School programs with small grants, summer programs and presentation slots. | Sometimes |
| **Undergraduate journals / conferences** | Where a finished paper can be submitted or presented. | No |

### 5.8 Working with professors

| Level | What they do | How it shows |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Feedback | Read a draft once or twice | Thanked in the first footnote |
| 2. Advisor | Meet regularly, guide the method | Acknowledged; can become an independent study |
| 3. Co-author | Shape design, analysis and writing | Both names (economics usually lists authors alphabetically) |
| 4. Grad student co-author | A PhD student works with you, often advised by their professor | Co-authors |

Start at level 1 with a short draft. Before any co-authorship, agree in writing on
data, ethics approval, the right to publish any result, who does what and author order.

### 5.9 Ethics
- Public, de-identified datasets usually don't need ethics approval. Check the dataset's terms.
- Collecting data from people (surveys, wearables, interviews) as university
  research usually needs approval from the school's ethics board (the IRB) first.
- Disclose any conflict of interest (for example, studying a product you founded).

### 5.10 What a strong paper looks like
- **Leads with a puzzle**, not a topic.
- **A question-style title** ("Did…?", "Pills or Push-ups?").
- **One striking chart** early.
- **A one-sentence finding** people can repeat.
- **Standard layout:** abstract, introduction, what we already know, data, method,
  results, limitations, conclusion. 10–20 pages for a first paper.
- **Honest limits.** A short, careful paper beats a long, shaky one.

## 8. Standards (apply to everything)

**Facts:**
- Every number has a source (authors, journal, year, link).
- **Tier A:** checked against the paper or official abstract. **Tier B:** checked against
  the journal abstract, a university press release or a major outlet. **Unchecked:** don't use it.
- Copy numbers exactly; round down, never up.
- "Linked to" for anything that isn't a randomized trial or natural experiment.
- State the main limit in one line.

**Known traps:**
| Popular claim | What's true |
|---|---|
| Gym members "waste $700" | $600 in the published paper ($700 was a 2002 draft) |
| Audiobooks raised gym visits 51% | Only at first; it faded |
| "Habits take 254 days" | A model estimate; the median was 66 days |
| One more hour of sleep a night raises pay 5% | It's one more hour **per week** |
| The megastudy tested 53 programs | The *Nature* paper says 54 |
| Exercise "makes you earn 6–10% more" | A correlation |
| "21 days to form a habit" | Not supported by the studies above |

**Integrity:**
- Disclose conflicts of interest.
- Never use individual-level data without consent and approval.
- Admit what a study can't show. It builds more credibility than it costs.

**Writing:** plain words, short sentences, one idea per piece, the most interesting thing first.
